¶1 Main Idea:
¶1The Everyday Effort Trap vs. Mechanical Work
In daily speech, we use the word "work" for anything that makes us sweat or feels exhausting. Imagine holding a heavy 500 N barbell completely still over your head for sixty seconds, or leaning with all your strength against a stalled car that refuses to roll. Within seconds, your arms shake and your muscles feel drained—yet physics records zero mechanical work in both situations. In physics, Mechanical Work is not a measure of human tiredness; it strictly measures energy transferred when an applied force actually moves an object through a displacement. If the object moves zero distance (d = 0 m), zero mechanical work is transferred to that object (W = 0 J). 		¶1 Annotation / Q:
¶2 Main Idea:
¶2The Mathematical Rule & Measuring in Joules
Whenever a steady force pushes or pulls in the direction of motion, mechanical work equals the product of parallel force and displacement: W = F · d. Force is measured in Newtons (N) and displacement in meters (m), giving the SI unit of work and energy: the Joule (J), where 1 J = 1 N · m. To feel one Joule, imagine picking up a small 0.10 kg apple. Using classroom gravity g = 10 m/s², its weight is 0.10 kg × 10 m/s² = 1.0 N. Lifting that 1.0 N apple straight up by 1.0 m transfers exactly 1.0 J of mechanical work. 		¶2 Annotation / Q:
¶3 Main Idea:
¶3Why Direction Rules: Positive (+W), Negative (-W), & Zero (0 J)
Because both force and displacement have direction, comparing their arrows reveals whether energy enters or leaves an object. First, when force points in the same direction as motion (pushing a sled forward), work is positive (+W) and adds energy. Second, when force points in the opposite direction to motion (friction acting backward on a sliding puck, or car brakes fighting forward travel), work is negative (-W) and removes mechanical energy. Third, when force points perpendicular (90°) to motion, work is zero (0 J). Carrying a heavy backpack forward across a flat hallway requires an upward force from your shoulders while you move horizontally—since vertical force never helps sideways motion, that carry force does 0 J of work! 		¶3 Annotation / Q:

¶4 Main Idea:
¶4Lifting Straight Up Against Gravity (W = m · g · h)
Raising an object upward at steady speed requires your upward lifting force to match the downward pull of gravity (Flift = Fg = m · g, using g = 10 m/s²). Because both lifting force and vertical height h point straight up, total lifting work equals Wlift = m · g · h. Lifting a 5.0 kg box (50 N) onto a 2.0 m high shelf transfers 50 N × 2.0 m = 100 J. 		¶4 Annotation / Q:
¶5 Main Idea:
¶5Simple Machines & The Force–Distance Trade-Off
How can one person load a heavy motorcycle onto a truck bed without superhuman strength? Ramps and multi-strand pulleys lower the required pull force Fin by spreading that same lift over a proportionally longer distance din. Without friction, ideal machines conserve work: Fin · din = Fout · dout. Making a ramp four times longer cuts the push force to one-fourth! 		¶5 Annotation / Q:
